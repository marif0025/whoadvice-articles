### Why someone may still want a digital clock

Use these as decision-led reasons, not a generic history lesson:

- It keeps the time visible at a glance without opening a phone and encountering messages or other notifications.
- A purpose-sized display can be read from bed, across a desk, or from the back of a shared room.
- A dedicated alarm clock can move the phone away from the bed while retaining a reliable wake-up tool.
- A classroom or office wall clock gives everyone one shared reference time.
- Some products preserve time or alarm settings through an outage; others automatically synchronize when their radio signal is available.

Do not claim that a dedicated digital clock improves sleep, focus, productivity, or learning unless suitable evidence is added. Present reduced phone interaction as a practical design benefit, not a measured health outcome.

### Secondary questions

- LED or unlit LCD?
- How dim does the display actually get, and can it turn fully off?
- How large should the digits be for the intended viewing distance?
- Battery-powered, plug-in, or plug-in with backup?
- Does “atomic” or radio-controlled time work in the reader's region and placement?
- Is the reader buying a clock, an alarm clock, or a countdown timer?
- Can an existing screen replace a physical clock?

### Common mistakes

- Comparing case or screen width as if it were digit height.
- Assuming “dimmable” means dark enough for a light-sensitive sleeper.
- Assuming backup batteries keep an LED display lit; some preserve settings or alarm behavior only.
- Buying a bright corded wall display where no outlet is conveniently located.
- Treating a digital time display as a classroom countdown timer.
- Calling radio-controlled time “always accurate” without noting signal, region, building, and setup limits.

Community material is anecdotal and is used to form questions, not prove performance.
